01 · Start here

What is SBCD?

School-Based Curriculum Development (SBCD) is a process through which a school community—often including teachers, school leaders, learners, families and relevant community partners—takes an active role in designing, adapting, implementing and evaluating curriculum in response to learners’ needs and local circumstances.

Key idea: SBCD is not simply choosing classroom activities. It involves purposeful curriculum decision-making: identifying needs, setting goals, selecting content and pedagogy, organising learning, assessing outcomes and revising the curriculum using evidence.

A useful framework

The SBCD cycle

SBCD is best understood as an iterative cycle rather than a one-off document-writing exercise.

StageGuiding questionPossible evidence
1. DiagnoseWhat do our learners and community need?Assessment data, learner voice, community context
2. DesignWhat should learners know and be able to do?Learning outcomes, curriculum map, planned units
3. ImplementWhich teaching and learning approaches fit?Lesson plans, learning resources, teacher collaboration
4. EvaluateWhat is working, for whom, and why?Student work, formative assessment, feedback
5. RefineWhat should we retain, adapt or discontinue?Review meeting, revised plans, action steps
Centralised and school-based

Compare the emphasis

Centralised emphasis

Major curriculum decisions are made primarily by a central authority, supporting consistency across schools.

SBCD emphasis

Schools and teachers have meaningful agency to interpret, contextualise or develop curriculum within relevant national or system requirements.

In practice, most systems combine central expectations with some local discretion. SBCD does not necessarily mean complete school autonomy.

02 · Core concepts

Principles of SBCD

These principles are a practical synthesis for analysing SBCD. Terminology and emphasis can vary across countries and theoretical traditions.

1

Needs-based and learner-centred

Curriculum choices respond to learners’ prior knowledge, interests, strengths, barriers and developmental needs.

Ask: What evidence tells us what learners need?

2

Contextual relevance

Learning connects with local culture, language, environment, community knowledge and real-world issues while maintaining broad educational aims.

Ask: How can local examples deepen—not narrow—learning?

3

Teacher agency and professionalism

Teachers use subject knowledge, pedagogical judgement and evidence to adapt curriculum collaboratively and responsibly.

Ask: What professional learning do teachers need?

4

Participation and collaboration

Curriculum decisions benefit from dialogue among teachers, leaders, learners, families and community stakeholders, with roles clearly defined.

Ask: Whose perspectives are missing?

5

Coherence and alignment

Outcomes, content, teaching approaches and assessment reinforce one another and align with applicable standards and progression across year levels.

Ask: Do assessments measure the intended outcomes?

6

Inclusion and equity

Curriculum design anticipates different learning needs and avoids treating local adaptation as a reason to lower expectations or exclude learners.

Ask: Who may benefit less from this design?

7

Evidence-informed evaluation

Schools review learner progress, teaching experiences and stakeholder feedback to improve curriculum decisions over time.

Ask: What evidence would justify changing the unit?

8

Feasibility and sustainability

Plans consider teacher workload, time, resources, leadership support and realistic implementation conditions.

Ask: Can the school sustain this beyond a pilot?

9

Reflective, continuous improvement

SBCD treats curriculum as revisable: teams learn from implementation, document decisions and improve the next cycle.

Ask: What will we change next time—and why?

03 · Historical background

How did SBCD develop?

SBCD did not emerge at one universal moment. Its development differs by national context, but several broad trends help explain its growth.

Early to mid-20th century · Central planning and standardisation

Many education systems relied heavily on centrally prescribed syllabuses, textbooks and examinations. Central direction was associated with consistency, nation-building and quality control, but could leave limited room for local adaptation.

Progressive education and curriculum inquiry · Early to mid-20th century

Progressive and child-centred approaches, including the work of John Dewey, emphasised experience, inquiry and the relationship between school learning and democratic life. These ideas helped strengthen arguments for attending to learners and context.

1960s–1970s · Curriculum reform and teacher participation

Curriculum reform movements gave greater attention to curriculum planning as a professional and educational process. Work associated with curriculum theorists such as Lawrence Stenhouse highlighted teachers’ roles in inquiry, interpretation and curriculum development.

1980s–1990s · School autonomy and site-based management

In a number of systems, decentralisation and school-improvement reforms increased interest in decisions made closer to schools. SBCD became linked to teacher collaboration, school leadership, local responsiveness and professional development.

2000s · Accountability, standards and evidence

Standards-based reform and stronger accountability requirements created a dual challenge: schools were encouraged to adapt learning to local needs while demonstrating alignment with national standards and measurable outcomes.

2010s–today · Competencies, inclusion and changing contexts

Curriculum work increasingly addresses digital learning, global and local issues, learner diversity, transferable competencies and community relevance. SBCD remains shaped by tensions between flexibility, equity, accountability and school capacity.

Historical interpretation: The broad movement is not a simple replacement of central control by school freedom. Contemporary systems commonly negotiate a balance between shared curriculum expectations and context-sensitive decisions.

04 · Apply your understanding

Mini case study

A secondary school notices that learners can recall environmental science terms but struggle to apply them to local problems. Teachers propose a cross-curricular unit on water quality using local data, science concepts, mathematics and persuasive writing. The school must align the unit with required learning standards and ensure all learners can participate.

Discuss with a study group

  1. What learner or community needs should be investigated before designing the unit?
  2. Which stakeholders should contribute, and how will learner voice be included?
  3. How could the team align outcomes, learning activities and assessment?
  4. What evidence would show whether the unit improved learning?
  5. What constraints or equity risks should be considered?

MEd extension: identify one tension between local relevance and system-wide consistency, then propose a reasoned response.

06 · MEd-level reflection

From understanding to critique

Write a short critical response to the following prompt:

“SBCD can make curriculum more responsive, but it may also reproduce inequalities between schools.” Discuss.

In your response, consider school resources, teacher expertise, leadership, stakeholder participation, accountability and mechanisms for protecting equity. Support your position with course readings and policy evidence.

Suggested starting points

Further reading

  • Dewey, J. (1916). Democracy and Education. A foundational account of experience, education and democratic life.
  • Tyler, R. W. (1949). Basic Principles of Curriculum and Instruction. A classic framework for curriculum planning and evaluation.
  • Stenhouse, L. (1975). An Introduction to Curriculum Research and Development. A key text on curriculum process and teacher inquiry.
  • OECD curriculum and school-improvement publications, alongside your national or state curriculum policy documents.
